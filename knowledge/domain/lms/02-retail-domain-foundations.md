## Roles & Responsibilities
The merchandise assortment building process requires many cross-functional teams (CFTs) that all play an important role.  In this section we will review these roles and their contributions to the process.
Keep in mind, every Retailer is different.  Some retailers may not have all of these roles;  Some retailers may have the responsibilities.   A great tool that can be leveraged by Retailers is a  “RACI” chart that details roles and responsibilities.

## Design Led vs Merchant Led Organizations
Organizations that are vertically integrated are responsible for the design of their own products, i.e. their “private label”.   This means the retailer is fully accountable for what products it is choosing to carry, and controls all aspects of product procurement:  from Design, Fabrication, Colors, Patterns, Trends that are being represented, etc.
With fashion retailers specifically, there are typically 2 types of paradigms that these teams follow in the Assortment Planning process:
Paradigm 1:  Design Led Process
In this paradigm, the Design Team has control of the product assortment strategy.  The Design team is responsible for DEFINING the trends and the product strategy.  Tory Burch is an example of this paradigm.  At Tory Burch, the Design Team designs a line based on the trends/vision, and they SET the strategy for the company (and buying teams), without being confined to inputs from buyers, merchants, or planners.
Paradigm 2:  Merchant Led Process
In this paradigm, the Merchant Team has control over what the product strategy is.  The Merchant Team INSTRUCTS the design team as to how many choices they need to design and what the attribute mix of the products needs to be.   For example, Express follows a a Merchant Led Process.

## What are Omni Channel and Multi Channel Paradigms?
Omni Channel refers to a paradigm where channels are combined for planning purposes.
Multi-Channel refers to the paradigm where channels are planned separately.  This is a more traditional, silo'd approach.
The key difference is related to how teams THINK about the assortment.  Do teams want a holistic experience for customers, no matter where they choose to shop?  As the lines between physical and digital retail become increasingly blurred, most retailers are taking a more “Omni-Channel” approach to provide the most seamless experiences for customers.  Additionally, an Omni-Channel approach support modern shopping paradigms such as “Buy Online Pick Up in Store (BOPIS)” much more seamlessly than traditional multi-channel models, where channels are planned in silos.
While it may seem that Omni Channel Models may be the “right” thing from a customer-centricity perspective, they also introduce complexities that need to be considered in Assortment Planning.
In this unit, omni channel models and nuances that must considered during the retail planning process will be reviewed.

## Omni Channel Inventory Models
A key consideration in Omni Channel models is how inventory is planned. In Omni Channel models, it is assumed that channels are planned “Together”, and thus one may assume they are “sharing” inventory from a common pool.
For example, a pure Omni Channel model believes it is servicing the customer no matter where and how they shop, and aims to ensure the inventory to support those sales is “available” across whichever channels they will shop (ie, ECOM vs Stores).
Some Omni models are more of a hybrid, where channels are planned for certain metrics (ie sales are planned ECOM vs Stores) but Inventory is pooled together.  This type of model can introduce complexity, as Retailers are in a dual state of needing to parse out certain metrics by Channel, while planning inventory as a  Shared Pool.
In order to reduce this complexity, some retailers utilize a concept of “Virtual DCs (VDCs)”.  Virtual DCs allow for a “logical” segmentation of inventory - not a physical one.  This means, ECOM teams can place product in “reserve” to make sure they have enough inventory to support events (for example).  Whereas Stores teams can place product in reserve, to ensure they have enough inventory to fill fixtures, and even prepare for new store openings (for example).

## Omni Channel Fulfillment Models
Another consideration for consumer-centric omni-channel models is how to support customer preferences for how she places orders and receives her order.   The most straighforward examples of this are 1) a customer buys and order online in ECOM, and ECOM ships the goods to her shipping address, and 2) a customer goes into a store and buys goods at the register and brings the home.
However, as a customer yourself, you may have also bought from retailers using different methods.  Have you ever used "Buy Online Pick Up in Store" services?  This is a great example of how retailers are seamlessly crossing channels to meet customers on their own terms.
While convenient and consumer centric, these cross channel fulfillment models introduce complexity that must be considered as part of assortment sales and inventory planning.
Below are some cases and complexities introduced that need to be considered:
Omni Case: BOPIS
What:
BOPIS stands for "Buy Online, Pick Up In Store".  In BOPIS scenarios, the customer places an order online and "buys" the product online, but picks up the inventory from the physical store.
Challenges:
Which channel gets credit for the sale?  Is it ECOM, or Stores? [In Most Cases, it is “ECOM”]
When planning next year, how do I plan to have Inventory in a physical store, without the sales attached to that store (because, the sales posted to ECOM?)
Solution:
S5 Assortment supports cross channel inventory management, where a % of sales in one channel can be diverted to another channel “behind the scenes” to ensure inventory is properly allocated.
Omni Case: BOSS
What:
BOSS stands for “Buy Online, Ship to Store”.  In BOSS scenarios, the customer places an online order, and the order is fulfilled not from the DC, but is shipped directly to a nearby physical store.
Challenges:
Similar to BOPIS - When planning next year, how do I plan to have Inventory in a physical store, without the sales attached to that store (because, the sales posted to ECOM?)
Solution:
S5 Assortment supports cross-channel inventory management, where a % of sales in one channel can be diverted to another channel “behind the scenes” to ensure inventory is properly allocated.
Omni Case: Cross Channel Returns to Inventory
What:
In this scenario, a Customer purchases products online, but returns them to Store.
Challenges:
Stores have inventory being returned that did not originate from their store, that adds to their inventory levels (and possibly sales), and also may reduce the need for products being allocated from the DC to that particular store.  How does S5 plan for this extra inventory from another channel?
Return “actual” data may post to Ecommerce, as that is the channel where the sale originated from.  How does S5 plan and account for the amount of returns being generated by channel?
Solution:
S5 Assortment allows users to plan for Cross-Channel Returns in Omni Channel implementations.  In ECOM, users will plan a “% Return to Stores”.  The system will then move that quantity to the Stores inventory buckets, thus crediting stores with extra inventory

## Omni Channel Pricing Strategies
Some Retailers decide execute different pricing strategies (either PROMOs, or Ticket Prices themselves) by channel.  Retailers may think this allows them flexibility to gain margin or localize pricing to adapt to market price sensitivity (or lack thereof).
From a consumer centric POV, executing a strategy where Ticket Prices are different by [selling channel] and even by [location] can be controversial.  What if the same customer is shopping online and sees a product is $40, but then goes to the store and the same product is $50?  Alternatively, what if a customer went to store 1 and saw the product was $50, and then traveled a few miles to store 2 and saw the product was $40?  Many times, even though pricing can be managed at lower levels, it may not equate to the best customer experience and may even turn customers away.

## Currency Conversion
For businesses with global operations, when "sales" happen, they actualize in the "Local" Currency (ie sales are in Euros in Europe, GBP in the UK).   Although sales come into the org in the "Local" currency, corporate offices typically need to report on a common, universal currency, usually in the country where the corporation is registered.  This common, reported currency is known as "base currency".   For example, even though Tory Burch does business in Japan, they will ultimately state their sales results for Japan in US Dollars.  Generally, corporate financial reporting is in Base Currency, not Local Currency.
When creating plans, International Planning teams are interested in stating plans in both the "Local" currency (to gain a consumer centric view), as well as "Base" currency (for financial reporting).
The process of currency conversion is a relatively straightforward concept.   A exchange rate (also known as fx rate), is applied to values in local currency, which calculates the value in Base currency.
But, fx rates can change over time, which can create complexity when trying to understand differences between a plan for this year, and the comparison basis used for LY and LLY.   If time-phased fx rates are used to state LY and LLY values in base currency, it may appear that there is "growth" or "decline" in sales values, where infact, the local value is constant.
The best approach in this case is to "Rebase" history.  Rebasing is where the TY fx rates are applied to all local values for LY and LLY to be used for comparison.  When fx rates are "rebased", it helps preserve true variances to LY in local/base currency, as opposed to variances that are a result of fx rate changes.

## Ticket Prices - International
International businesses have different consumer protections in place that must be respected.  Some of those are regarding Ticket Price.  In the US, a ticket price reflects the price paid before taxes.  However, in some international regions, the ticket price must reflect the total price the customer will pay, including taxes.
For example, International rules apply VAT (Value Added Taxes) to certain products’ ticket prices.  Unlike in the US where taxes are applied on top of the ticket price, in Europe VAT is included in the ticket price.   Meaning, if planning the same base ticket price for an item (100), in the US, the ticket would read (100) but in EU the ticket price may read (110).
VAT has different rates applied based on product types and sizes.  For example, for a child’s shirt, VAT may be applied to sizes 4T, 5T, and 6T, but not for sizes 9 Months, 12 Months, and 24 Months.
Additionally, some international markets apply premiums to the ticket price, which is an increase in the ticket price based on the market's price sensitivity (or lack thereof).  For example, the same dress offered in the UK at 110 may be offered in Germany for 120,  because German customers are willing to pay more for dresses.
Other considerations, such as the physical price printed on a ticket, are also considered.  For example, a product selling in both the US and Canada may have a ticket with both the US Dollar and Canadian Dollar prices printed on it.  For Assortment Planning, if a user decides to change the price “in the system”, we need to consider the impact to the “physical” tickets as well.
Ticket Price strategies where a product is a different price by [selling channel] and even by [location] can be controversial.  What if the same customer is shopping online and sees a product is $40, but then goes to the store and the same product is $50?  Alternatively, what if a customer went to store 1 and saw the product was $50, and then traveled a few miles to store 2 and saw the product was $40?  Many times, even though pricing can be managed at lower levels, it may not equate to the best customer experience and may even turn customers away.

## International Regulations
International businesses also have to respect and consider certain restrictions that exist in certain regions, as these can impact the decision to (or not to) offer products there.
Example 1:  Products marked as Hazmat cannot be shipped internationally.  (So, international websites, or Domestic websites that ship internationally, should not offer this product in their available assortment)
 Example 2:  The US restricts the sale of pajamas if they do not meet certain fire safety standards.  So, if a UK-based company should not assort non-compliant pajamas to their US market.

## International Selling Nuances
International also presents complexity in creating product assortments themselves, as customer preferences can vary wildly even in countries adjacent to one another.
This is where Assortment Planning can help!
For example:  In Germany, Women’s Outdoor Jackets start selling as early as August for the Fall season, whereas in the rest of Europe, Jacket selling season does not start until November.  (Germans buy in advance, where others buy based on need/impulse). Hence, a retailer should make sure to bring in the Jackets assortment early in Germany, as they may miss the peak selling season (even though it is not the peak ‘usage’ season!)

## ECOM vs Stores Nuances
Retailers need to account for different nuances and customer behavior across the different selling channels.  A great example of this is the different nuances between ECOM and Stores.  These nuances generally warrant different “plans” or “strategies” by channel, where teams will want to explicitly set targets or parameter values differently by channel.
Some examples are:
Product Performance
ECOM can see vast differences in top selling items vs physical stores.  The reasons could be many:
Different (“wider”) product assortment
Easier search capabilities
2D vs 3D experience with the garments.  For example, a top performing Black Sweater in stores may not sell well on ECOM, because the image does not capture product details (ie stitching, hand feel, weight of the yarn etc) that are captured easily in a physical experience.
Metrics
ECOM and stores may require different metrics to plan & evaluate performance.  Here are a few examples:
Stores leverage “Average per Store per Week” metrics (APS) to evaluate how many of each product sells in an average store.  This metric helps store metrics “level the playing field”, as it allows products that were not carried in all stores to be evaluated with products that were.
In ECOM, the APS Metric doesn’t make much sense (as, ECOM itself is just “one big store”).  As a result, for ECOM, we use a metric called “Rate of Sale” (ROS) that is the Average Sales per Week (without the store divisor)
ECOM also uses analytic metrics such as Website Traffic and Page visits, whereas Stores use traditional Traffic metrics (if counters are available in physical stores)
ECOM teams analyze and plan for “Demand”, which is an order placed but not shipped from the warehouse.  (Demand is not a valid concept in stores.. Because you simply buy the product at the register = instant shipment!)
In the same vein, ECOM teams also analyze Backorders, which are Orders that have not yet shipped, as well as Cancellations, which are orders that either a customer cancels, or the retailer cancels (ie, as a result of taking an order for a product that has become out of stock).

## Returns ECOM vs Stores
Returns are a known factor in retail and need to be accounted for.  There are differences in return processing for stores and ECOM, including the following:
Return Processing - differences between ECOM vs Stores
There are some operational differences between returns made to a store, vs those returned to ECOM/DC.
For stores, many times the stores can ‘process’ the returned garments and have them ready for resale (ie steaming the garment, adding tickets, etc)
For ECOM, returns processing can have a few more steps:
Step 1:  Return notification generated (ie UPS Shipping Slip)
Step 2:  Goods are physically received in the DC -  they do not show as available to sell in inventory yet.
Step 3:  Goods are processed and made ready for sale for ECOM (cleaned, checked, put back into poly bags etc) - still not showing as available to sell in inventory yet
Step 4:  Products are booked back into inventory and available for resale.  During these steps, a product might not make it through all the steps.  For example, imagine a product was received damaged or no longer in a resalable condition.  For these reasons, when we work with retailers, we ask them to send us return data for when the products get booked back into inventory.
Return Rates - ECOM vs Store
Stores vs ECOM may have different return rates, just based on the nature of the business model.
ECOM selling channels can return rates can be upwards of 30% or more.  (Have you ever ordered the same garment in multiple sizes, and returned the ones that didn't fit?)  A 30% return rate means, 1 out of every 3rd item sold will come back into inventory
Whereas stores may naturally have a lower return rate (because, presumably the customer had tried on and had physically seen the garment before they decided to purchase it)

## Purpose of Hierarchies
Hierarchies provide a definition of a product across predetermined levels of parentage.  Hierarchies allow for categorization of items in an organized structure.  Hierarchies also ensure items are classified in an existing structure that is unified across the organization.  Hierarchies are critical for master data management, product ordering, and analysis.
The parent-child structure of "hierarchies" is applied to multiple dimensions:  Product, Location, Time, for example.
In hierarchies, data follows a strict “parent-child” relationship (ancestry).  A child can only have one parent, but a parent can have more than one child.  This structure ensures a clean classification of data.
Good Hierarchies have the following characteristics:
Strict “parent-child” relationship
Presence of an ID and a Name for each member.
IDs that are guaranteed to be unique across the entire data set.  IDs serve as the “Key” across all data.
No duplicate IDs
IDs that do not change for a given member.
A strict process for ID purging/removal that is no longer in use (usually, after 7 years after activity for the member has ceased)
A strict and controlled process for adding of members or changing of the hierarchy structure (ie changing the # of levels)
All products, location, and time members are defined as members in a hierarchy (with exception of DCs in some cases)
A record’s hierarchy must be completely filled out at all levels;  Meaning, each member defined has a complete parentage throughout all levels above it and below it, if applicable.

Assortment Planning - Floorset Paradigm
Floorsets are named periods of time that help Retailers plan their business in a more meaningful way than just using the Fiscal calendar.   Floorsets allow retailers to execute assortments in unique groupings of weeks that will help better align with the customer or industry.
This section will provide the following information:  What are Floorsets, How do retailers use Floorsets,  what are the benefits that Floorsets provide, and what potential challenges do Floorsets introduce.

## What are Floorsets?
A standard fiscal calendar contains the values of "week, month, quarter, half, year".   For some retailers, planning assortments using a standard fiscal calendar is just fine.  A retailer that uses the fiscal calendar can plan different product drops over time, typically varying assortments by Month or Quarter.  For example, these retailers may have 12 "drops" a year:  "May" drop, and then an "April" drop, etc, in which they will introduce new products, and possibly phase out others.  Their teams are organized to expect new "drops" every fiscal month.
For other retailers, the fiscal calendar is too rigid and does not align with how they want to plan their assortment product launches.  Launching product by Month may not be frequent enough, or may be too frequent.    It is for these cases that the concept of Floorset exists.   A fast fashion retailer may want to think of varying their assortment by "design seasons", such as Spring, Summer, Autumn, Winter, and Resort.   This may provide a more consumer centric, and contextual assortment, based on time of year, that a fiscal calendar does not provide.   In this example, retailers are launching collections less frequently than "month", but more frequently than "quarter".  Additionally the definition of "Spring" is agnostic to Month.  It may be "Week 3 to Week 14", which straddles months and quarters and even halfs.
In another example, a retailer may think that Monthly drops are too infrequent.  For example, a retailer that is fast fashion may need to launch new collections more frequently than a season or a month.  These retailers may drop products every 3 weeks or so, and would create floorsets called "Spring I, Spring II, Spring III, Summer I, Summer II, Summer III.. and so on" -- effectively organizing weeks of a year into 16 distinct floorset periods by which they will plan and change their assortments.
In summary,  Floorset periods provide Retailers with flexibility to define their assortment calendar based on how they want to best service their customers.

## Floorset Period - Sales and Receipt Period Definitions
In addition to defining the number of floorsets - or, in other words, defining the floorset for which each week belongs - Retailers also like to summarize data for the floorsets in specific ways.
As one may know, Retailers can only sell products if they have inventory.  Inventory needs to be received in order for a retailer to sell.  This process of "receiving product" or "receipts" involves receiving Purchase Orders at the DC (Distribution center / Warehouse), creating stock transfer orders that tell the Warehouse what quantities to send to which stores, and actually sending cartons full of inventory via trucks that leave the warehouse and go to the stores.
This process of "receiving" goods in the DC, and "allocating" goods from the DC to stores can take up to a week or more to execute.  As such, Retailers need to plan for receipts weeks ahead of when they want to start selling products, in order to ensure inventory is in stores in time for the Floorset start.
When a retailer wants to plan Sales and Receipts needed to execute an assortment for a floorset,  they typically need to account for this offset in time between the Receipt period (in the DC) and the Sales start period.
To support this, S5 provides retailers with ways that they can define different Receipt and Sales periods to facilitate rollups and analysis.  For example: Assume there is a floorset called Spring I.  The Retailer wants the assortment for Spring I to sell between Feb Wk 1 to Mar Wk 3.   If a user pulled a report for Feb Wk 1 to Mar Wk 3, she would show values for SALES, but she would NOT show values for RECEIPTS, since the receipts needed to come in PRIOR TO the sales start, in order to be available in store in time.
As such, Retailers want to pull a report that pulls different weeks, based on the metric.  For our Spring I example above, the actual request for a user, when pulling a report fo Spring I Assortment, is to show:
Sales = Pull data from Feb Wk 1 thru Mar Wk 3
Receipts = Pull data from Jan Wk 1 thru Jan Wk 4.
This is a critical concept for Floorset planning.

## Purpose of Attributes
Attributes are characteristics assigned to products that help describe the product in a structured way.  Attributes are used for planning and analysis.
Best practice for attributes are as follows:
A finite set of attributes are defined and maintained
Leadership has defined important, key attributes that are mandatory across the org.
The “valid values” for a given attribute are clear, unique values and are not duplicate or ambiguous.
For example, under an attribute called “Material”.  If there were values of “Gold”, “Silver”, and “Metal”, it may be hard for a user to know if they should code a gold bracelet using “Gold” or using “Metal” -- as the bracelet has both of these traits.
Attributes and their values are managed in a structured, controlled way.
There is a process to review requests for new attributes or members.
There is a team that serves as a gatekeeper, to review requests and approve.
For example: A merchant may request a new attribute called “Sleeve Length”, without knowing that Sleeve Length values already exist under another attribute called “Length”.
There are protections against users “hand-keying” values.
This keeps the data “clean”;  Hand Keying can lead to things like different “cases”  (ie WHITE vs White vs white), trailing spaces (such as “White  “) etc.

## Types of Attributes
There are different types of attributes:
Product Attributes:
These are attributes used to describe product characteristics.
Product attributes can be assigned at multiple levels:  Style, StyleColor, and Size/SKU.  As a best practice, attributes are assigned at the level at which differences should be coded.
For example, for a T-Shirt Style, the “Sleeve Length” attribute makes sense to code at the Style level, since all Colors and Sizes will by definition have the same sleeve length as the style.
An attribute for Color Family should be coded at the Style Color Level.  Coding at the Style level wouldn't make sense, since color values for members below Style will vary.
An attribute for Size family should be coded at the Style Color Size (SKU) level.  Coding at the Style or Style Color level would not make sense, since Size Family values below Color will vary.
Examples of Product Attributes:
Color
Silhouette
Price Band
Sleeve Length
Neckline
Etc Etc
Location Attributes:
These are attributes used to describe Locations.
Some examples of Location Attributes are:
Capacity
Climate
Format (Mall, Main Street etc)

## Lifecycle
Lifecycle is the pattern of demand that is shaped purely by an item's planned lifespan.  As a customer, you would possibly expect that an item that is newly introduced may be more "exciting" during its initial debut, vs 6 months into its life.  As such, lifecycle can be considered in terms of "newness" curve,  and can very much be considered as the influence that the "newness factor" has on a product's sales over time, week by week.
In the fashion apparel world specifically, lifecycle is a very important aspect.   For fast fashion buys, trends can peak and trough significantly, as something that was once "hot" (new) is now "not" (old & out of style).

## Seasonality
Seasonality is the demand pattern that predicts less about a given item's ability to sell, but more focuses on the ability for the week itself to generate sales.
For example - weeks leading up to Christmas will naturally tend to have higher sales than will weeks in February, for example.  The cause of this is not due to the products; it is due to increased shoppers in the market ("Traffic"). As such, a good definition of Seasonality is that it is a proxy for “traffic” or the expected “attention”or “appetite” that will be inherent in the given week or time of year, and hence, an increased or decreased ability to sell more (or less).
In general, seasonality patterns are defined at Brand, Department, Class, and/or Subclass Levels.  This helps capture nuances based on types of products based on time of year.  (Shorts will have more demand in Summer months vs Winter months;  Winter coats will have more demand in winter months vs summer;  and so on)
Seasonal curves are expressed as each week’s  “percent of average” or "index" compared to the average of the year.  This means, a year will average out to a 1.0;  but weeks within may vary 1.2x average, or 0.8x average based on the expected variance to the average for an average week of a year.

Fashion

Basics
Basics are products that are planned to live for more than 26 weeks, and very likely are carried for years, and even have been carried for multiple years.  Based on this definition, Basics are generally not too “trendy” by definition;  they are classic or essential products that stand the test of time.
For example, a simple white t-shirt or black cardigan that are essential outfitting pieces and can “live” with any assortment that may be introduced.

Assortment Planning - Pricing, Price Status & Markdowns
Assigning a ticket price for an item is an important part of Assortment Planning.  As anyone who is a consumer themselves would know, a product’s price can impact a customer's decision to purchase.   The “feeling” a consumer has for the price being offered for the product is known as “perceived value”.  If a consumer “feels” a price is too high in relationship to the item on offer, they are less likely to purchase it.
Retailers assign a “ticket price” (or MSRP) to an item, which is the starting point for how much the item is on offer for.  However, the ticket price is not necessarily what the customer will pay "out the door".  If a retail is running a promotional discount (ie 30% off), the promotion will be applied to the ticket price, and the customer's final price will be lower than the original ticket.

## Retail Pricing Strategies
Retailers may subscribe to different strategies when it comes to setting ticket prices and / or when they determine how promotional they will be.
A few typical strategies are listed below, and may be used by retailers as part of their business model:
High-Low Strategies
These strategies rely on and depend on promotions as part of the selling strategy.  A prime example of this is Kohls.  If you are a Kohls shopper, you are likely trained
too use coupons, “Kohls Cash”, and look for promotions on products.
On the extreme end where promotions will be frequent, such as Kohls, often this strategy employs a ticket price that is built to provide a perception of a “deal” or discount. The
idea is to provide customers with a feeling that they are getting a bargain, when the compare how much they paid for an item, vs the item's ticket price.
Most retailers are a little less extreme, where there is a more reasonable MSRP is set, but there is some plan for at least a few promotions (sales) that are “pulsed” throughout
the year.
In Summary, the ticket price is set, but the retailer is planning that the price the customer will pay will be lower.
EDLP (Every Day Low Price)
Every Day Low Price strategies are where the price generally “is what it is”, or "what you see is what you get".
Walmart is a great example of EDLP pricing strategy.  Instead of playing the ‘high-low’ game, these retailers operate with a more straightforward approach, where they offer
the lowest ticket price without the game of coupons etc.
MAP Pricing Strategies (Branded Products)
MAP pricing strategies are followed by retailers that offer products from 3rd party vendors (brands).   For these products, the Vendor/Brand sets the MSRP (ticket price), and
also defines when the retailer is allowed to offer the product at a discount.  MAP rules are designed to allow the Brand to control the brand’s integrity (ie, don’t dilute
the brand’s image by offering 50-70% discounts on premium products).  MAP pricing rules also ensure specific retailers aren’t promoting things differently than other
retailers (ie, to ensure Macy’s isn't promoting a certain Nike style that Nordstrom also carries… which could possibly hurt sales at Nordstrom unfairly)
MAP pricing rules are set for both times (weeks) a product can be promoted, as well as ‘how deep’ the discount can be.
MAP pricing rules also include a MAP “break” date, usually near the end of a product’s full price life, where the rules no longer apply and the Retailer can set whatever price
they see fit.
Markdowns
“Markdowns” is the term used to denote the difference between the ticket price, and the price the customer actually paid.

## These are different “types” of markdowns that retailers strategize:
Point of Sale Markdowns (POS Markdowns)
Permanent Markdowns (Perm Markdowns)

## Point of Sale Markdowns (POS Markdowns)
Point of Sale Markdowns are price reductions that are performed or ‘rung up’ at the register.  With POS Markdowns, there is no change to the ticket price itself;  there are discounts being applied to the ticket price in order to reduce the price the customer will actually pay. These discounts can come in a few forms:
Coupons / Promo Codes:
These are discounts for the customer where the final price is reduced based on the coupon’s value
 (example: “Take 10% off any one item”)
Promotions:
Promotions are reductions of the final price that are set by the Retailer as an “event” and are pushed down to the register to ‘automatically’ reduce the item’s selling price.
(example:  “Take 20% off select denim styles”)
Employee Discounts
Discounts that are offered to employees as a ‘perk’.  Employee discounts may have some restrictions.
(example:  “40% off all full price items, 20% off all permanently marked down products”)
Military/Teacher/Senior etc Discounts
Discounts that apply as a perk to certain members of the community.
(example: “10% off your purchase if you present a valid military ID”)
Appeasements
Discounts applied at a manager’s discretion in part to appease a customer, to correct a poor customer experience, or to liquidate defective products (example:  a manager offers a customer a price at “50% off" for a white sweater that is missing a button”;  or, the retailer offers a customer 10% off her next purchase,  since her online order took over 2 weeks to deliver)

## Permanent Markdowns (Perm MDs)
Permanent Markdowns are a permanent change to a ticket price to a lower price.  Permanent Markdowns signify the “end” of a product’s “featured” and “funded” period -- and the primary objective changes to a Liquidation strategy.   Perm MDs usually operate with the following key concepts:
Perm MDs follow a “cadence”
Once permanently marked down, the product’s price will continue to be reduced permanently until the product is at it’s maxed allowed discount or until the product is liquidated
This is known as a Markdown Cadence
Typical markdown cadence can be:
MD1 = First Perm Markdown
MD2 = Second Perm MD
MD3 = Third Perm MD
MDC = Final “Clearance” Markdown
Each milestone is a ‘deeper’ reduction to the item's ticket price.
The Cadence also has a time dimension, with each deeper markdown happening at a scheduled time (such as, every 2 weeks)
Retailers use the verbiage as follows:
“INITIALS” = the initial markdown
“FURTHERS” = any subsequent price reduction after the initial ‘hit’
Perm MDs can have a labor ‘overhead’
For physical stores, there may be a requirement for the ticket price to be physically altered to reflect the new changed price.
Some retailers will use a red pen, others will stick an orange sticker on the ticket that reflects the new price.  Just a consideration, that there can be store labor involved in a price change (and hence, the cadence should not be too frequent, ie “daily” in order to be considerate of the effort involved at the store level)
Perm MDs should generally not be reversed
A product should not go from Perm MD back to Full Price.  In many cases this is “illegal”

## Price Status
Price Status is a status assignment for a style color, by week, that specifies if the item has undergone a permanent price change yet. Generally, retailers are interested in knowing if an item in a given week was permanently marked down, or not.   Many Retailers keep track of an item’s price status over time.   This can help the retailer understand what the product mix was (how many products sold at full price, vs those that were permanently marked down).  As a result, retailers will assign the value of “Full Price” to products in weeks prior to the Permanent Markdown week, and assign the value of “Markdown” for all weeks during and after Permanent markdown week.
Examples of Price Status Values:
Full Price - coded to any week for where a product has not undergone a permanent markdown
Mark Down - coded to any week after permanent markdown has occurred
Promo - This is a status occasionally used by some retailers.  “Promo” status is a subset of the Full Price Status.  Promo Status is coded to any week in the Full Price period where the retailer offered promotional discounts on a product.

## Price Elasticity of Demand
As a consumer yourself, you may already understand that a discount or promotion has an impact on the appeal of a customer to purchase.   The relationship between the ticket price and the discount offered, and the associated “lift” (increase) in sales, is known as “price elasticity of demand”.  Price elasticity essentially means: “For [x]% decrease in price, demand will increase [y]%”
Price elasticity is not always linear in nature.  For example, the lift associated with a 50% discount on an item that has a ticket price of $1 may be different from the lift associated with a 50% discount on an item that is $1,000.
Additionally, different product types may see different associated lifts from the same discount %.  For example, a $100 jewelry item may see a 1.5x lift for a 50% discount, whereas a $100 Womens Outerwear jacket may see a 5.0x lift from a 50% discount.
As such, in assortment planning, it is important to plan the expected discount percent as part of the items plan.  If the system knows that a discount is expected, it can predict the units that will be sold, and thus plan the inventory required to support the promotion.  If promotions are not planned, the receipts inventory planned by the system may not be sufficient to support the event (thus, resulting in missed sales and unhappy customers).
Price elasticity models are included in S5’s analytics platform.

In Assortment Planning, Purchase Orders are executed as a result of the “publish” action, which tells partner systems how much the merchant intends to buy for a product, at the SKU level. For many vertically -integrated retailers, the ability to reduce a purchase order may depend on the stage the product is in the manufacturing process.
For example, if the yarn/fabric has already been dyed and cut, the Retailer will have to still “own” that fabric, even if they cancel the order.  This is known as taking a “liability”, and the retailer will have to determine a) if they should cancel or b) if they cancel, how else will they use that fabric (maybe for another style)
Generally, retailers will not cancel orders without working it out with PD/Sourcing teams, as they will need to strike a balance between the retailers request, and keeping a good relationship with the supplier.

## Purchase Order Notifications (Advanced Shipping Notices)
Most suppliers will notify the retailer of the PO’s exact details once the PO has left their factory (xfcty) and/or has left the port.  This notification is known as an ASN (Advanced Shipment Notification).   For a PO that is being shipped from China via Sea/Boat, the ASN generally comes around 4 weeks prior to the delivery to the DC. For domestic orders, the ASN will come much faster, maybe days, or 1-2 weeks before.
Retailers use ASNs to do “pre-Allocations” of products prior to their receipts, which can speed up the distribution process once the items are received in the warehouse (DC).
